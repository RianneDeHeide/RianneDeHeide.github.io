Postdocs: developing into an independent researcher

A postdoc is a slightly strange career stage. You have finished your PhD and are expected to function as an independent researcher, while you are still in a temporary position that is often connected to somebody else's group or grant. At the same time, it can give you a few years in which research is still most of your job and you have considerably more freedom than during a PhD.

I think a postdoc should primarily be about your own development, so before accepting one I would ask what you want to know, what you want to become better at, and what you hope to have built by the time you leave.

1. Use the postdoc to learn something new

A postdoc can be a very good moment to move into an adjacent research area, close enough to your existing expertise that you can contribute from the start, while still being different enough that you have a lot to learn.

I saw my own postdocs partly in this way. After a PhD you know how to do research, but there is still an enormous amount you have never encountered, and a postdoc gives you the chance to enter a new area under the guidance of somebody who knows it much better than you do. At the same time, you bring enough of your own expertise to ask useful questions and make independent contributions.

That combination can broaden the kinds of problems you are able to work on for the rest of your career, and it can lead to a research direction that combines what you brought from the PhD with what you learned afterwards. When I look at a postdoc position, one of the questions I find most useful is therefore:

What will I know or be able to do after this postdoc that I cannot do now?

2. Choose a supervisor who gives you room

The postdoc mentor still matters enormously, especially when you are entering a new area. You want somebody from whom you can learn, who will discuss ideas with you, introduce you to the field, and tell you when you are overlooking something important, while also giving you enough freedom to follow questions that interest you.

I would be wary of a postdoc in which the PI has already designed a long list of tasks and mainly needs somebody to carry them out. I also do not think that is an ideal model for a PhD, but by the postdoc stage I would expect considerably more space to initiate work yourself and to decide which directions are worth pursuing.

Before accepting a position, I would therefore want to know things such as:

  • How much of the research programme is already fixed by the grant?

  • Can I start projects that are not directly in the grant proposal?

  • Can I collaborate independently with people inside and outside the group?

  • If we develop a new line together, what can I continue working on after I leave?

  • Does the PI see postdocs as researchers who are developing their own programme, or mainly as people hired to deliver a project?

If possible, talk to current and former postdocs as well. Former postdocs can tell you what they were able to build during the position, which parts of the work they could take with them, and how the supervisor supported them when they moved on.

3. Build something that is recognisably yours

It is completely normal to finish papers from the PhD after defending, and those papers may still be among the best work you have done. At the same time, I would hope that the postdoc gradually adds work that could not simply have been the next chapter of the thesis.

A rough picture that I find useful is to spend part of the time finishing good work from the PhD, part of it developing new work with people in the postdoc environment, and part of it starting at least one direction that feels recognisably your own. The balance between those three will differ from person to person and from one postdoc to another, but by the end I would like there to be some work for which your own questions and choices are clearly visible.

For me, independence is closely connected to that. You can still collaborate intensively with former supervisors, your postdoc mentor and many other people, while becoming increasingly able to initiate questions yourself, choose directions, see connections between projects, decide what seems worth pursuing, and explain where you would like your research to go next.

A useful diagnostic is: if somebody asks what you work on, is there an answer that does not start with the name of your PhD or postdoc supervisor?

4. Use the research time while you have it

Aaditya Ramdas, in his short note Why do a postdoc, and why do one at CMU?, makes two points I particularly like: a postdoc can provide both research time and contemplation time. For many people, this is the last stage of their academic career in which they will have so much time for research. Once you become an assistant professor, teaching, supervision, grant applications, committees and other responsibilities start taking up a much larger part of the week, and those demands tend to grow as your career progresses.

I would enjoy that freedom and use it quite broadly: read outside your immediate project, go to seminars, visit people, learn a theory that you do not need for the paper you are currently writing, spend several uninterrupted days on one difficult question, and start collaborations that may take years to mature. Having the time to follow a question simply because you find it interesting is a luxury that you may not have again to quite the same extent.

Of course, I would not spend the entire postdoc doing research and nothing else. Unless you are among the tiny handful of people producing truly groundbreaking papers, even very good research is unlikely to be enough on its own to get you a faculty position. In the Netherlands especially, academic jobs usually combine research with teaching, supervision and organisational responsibilities, and departments also care about hiring someone who will be a good colleague. I would therefore protect the research time that makes a postdoc so valuable, while also gaining some experience with other aspects of academic life, as I discuss in the next section.

The same advice about looking after the brain that has to do the thinking still applies; I wrote more about that on the During your PhD page.

5. Think of the postdoc as a junior assistant-professor stage

I often think of the postdoc as something like a junior assistant-professor stage in terms of professional development. Research should still be the main part of the job, while the position can also give you opportunities to build some of the broader experience that you may need for the next step.

If you hope to apply for assistant-professor positions in the Netherlands, for example, it is useful to look at actual vacancies while you are still a postdoc and see what departments ask for. In many cases that includes more than papers: some teaching, supervision, collaboration, service, grant-writing experience, and evidence that you can develop an independent research line.

A postdoc can be a good time to acquire some of that experience in a manageable way. You might teach part of a course, supervise a Bachelor's or Master's thesis, co-supervise a junior researcher, organise a seminar, help run a workshop, or contribute to a grant proposal. I particularly like thesis supervision as an opportunity because it gives you real mentoring experience without necessarily taking over a very large part of the postdoc.

I would choose these activities with some care and with your own next step in mind. If you want an academic career, a few well-chosen teaching, supervision or service activities can be useful additions to the research portfolio; if you are planning to leave academia, there is little reason to collect them simply because they look academic.

6. Build a network larger than one supervisor

A postdoc is also a good moment to move away from the idea that one supervisor should provide every kind of advice. The person who is best for discussing a proof may not be the person who understands grants, the Dutch academic job market or a particular application area, and somebody you meet elsewhere may become a collaborator for decades.

I would therefore try to use the whole environment around you. A famous department is of surprisingly little use if you spend two years talking only to the person whose name is on your contract, whereas seminars, reading groups, nearby departments and informal conversations can give you a much wider group of people to learn from and work with.

Moving to a new institution or field can be especially valuable for this reason, because it gives you access to another academic community and gradually helps you build a network that is less tied to the network of your PhD supervisor.

7. How do you find a postdoc?

There is no single postdoc market in Europe, and positions appear throughout the year, with different websites and mailing lists being more or less important depending on the field and country.

  • For positions in the Netherlands, AcademicTransfer is the main general vacancy site.

  • For research jobs across Europe, EURAXESS lists vacancies, funding opportunities and hosting offers.

  • Field-specific mailing lists, society pages, MathJobs, AcademicJobsOnline and conference networks can also be important, depending on your area.

Your network matters as well, so tell supervisors and collaborators that you are looking and talk to people at conferences. Many positions will eventually be advertised formally, although you may hear much earlier that a grant is coming or that somebody expects to recruit.

Interested in doing a postdoc with me?

I advertise all postdoc positions on this website, including positions that I expect to open in the future once I have received funding for them. There is therefore no need to email me to ask whether I currently have an opening, or whether I expect one soon: if I have funding for a future postdoc position, I will already have announced it here.

When I hire a postdoc, I am looking for a good match in both directions. I hope you will bring expertise and ideas that add something to my research, while also having a clear idea of what you would like to learn from working with me. I see a postdoc as a research collaboration in which I also have an important role as a mentor, helping you develop your own questions and become an independent researcher.

8. Bringing your own funding

A postdoc does not always have to start from an advertised position, because there are fellowships with which you can bring your own project and funding to a host institution. This can give you much more influence over the research question, the host and the direction of the project, while also giving you useful grant-writing experience early in your career.

At the European level, Marie Skłodowska-Curie Actions Postdoctoral Fellowships are an important route. There are also national and field-specific schemes, and the details change frequently enough that I would always check the current calls.

9. Rubicon

If you completed your PhD in the Netherlands, the NWO Rubicon programme is particularly worth knowing about. It is intended for researchers shortly after the PhD who want to gain research experience at another research institution, usually abroad, and you apply with your own project and host.

I had a Rubicon myself and used it for my postdoc at Inria Lille. Having my own grant gave me a great deal of freedom in choosing the research I wanted to do and was one of the things I valued most about that postdoc.

10. My own postdoc experience

I want to say something about my own postdoc period because it shaped quite strongly how I now think about this stage. I had chosen both of my postdoc places partly because I liked the supervisors and thought I could learn a lot from them, and scientifically there were good opportunities at both places, but personally I found those years much harder than I had expected.

After my PhD, I first did a postdoc of about nine months at Otto von Guericke University Magdeburg, working with Alexandra Carpentier. It started in the middle of the COVID lockdowns, and for almost the entire postdoc I was barely able to be at the university; only during the final few weeks could I work there in anything resembling a normal way. For much of that period I was alone in a small apartment in Magdeburg, and I also spent part of it back in the Netherlands, where I was again mostly alone in a small apartment. There was very little university life, almost no possibility of meeting new people, and hardly any way to build a social life in the city.

After Magdeburg I went to Inria Lille on my Rubicon. Scientifically, I felt very much at home in the research environment there, and the grant gave me a lot of freedom to choose what I wanted to work on. Living in France turned out to be much harder for me. I took French classes and put a lot of effort into learning the language, but I never managed to speak it well enough to integrate as much as I had hoped, which I found particularly frustrating because I already speak five other languages fluently and had expected that learning enough French to get by would be easier. COVID measures were still part of daily life as well, including distancing and many precautions around travelling, which made it even harder to build a normal social life.

Some research came out of both postdocs, although less than I had hoped for beforehand, and the whole period was much less enjoyable and productive than I had imagined. A large part of the difficulty was that I was trying to build a new research routine and a life in a new place while feeling quite isolated.

Before choosing Magdeburg and Lille, I had also seriously considered (and was offered) postdoc positions in the United States and Canada. The universities and potential supervisors were excellent, and several people advised me to go as far away as possible and choose the most prestigious place I could because they thought that would be best for an academic career in the Netherlands. I decided against that because I did not want to be so far away from friends and family, and in hindsight I am extremely glad that I made that choice: COVID started soon afterwards, and I would likely have spent a long period on the other side of the Atlantic with very little possibility of going home.

My own postdoc choices were therefore an attempt to find a balance between being in a strong research environment, working with supervisors from whom I expected to learn a lot, having enough independence to develop my own research, and staying close enough to home that life outside work still felt manageable. Even with those choices, I found the postdoc stage hard, and when I had the opportunity to return to the Netherlands for an assistant-professor position at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, I ended my Rubicon early and took the job. Coming back meant returning to my existing network of friends, family and hobbies while being able to continue my academic career, and for me that turned out to be a very good decision.

The pandemic obviously made those years unusual, so I do not know how I would have experienced the same postdocs in another period. I had lived abroad before, in Hamburg during my studies and in Umeå in Sweden, and had felt very much at home in both places, which is one reason I had not expected the postdoc years abroad to be so difficult.

What I took from the experience is that I now give the life around the job serious weight when thinking about job choices. The research environment, the supervisor and the academic opportunities matter a great deal, and so do friends, family, hobbies, language, community, distance from home, and whether you can imagine building an ordinary life in that place. I am very glad that I did not always follow the advice to optimise for prestige and distance, and my later career in the Netherlands also reassured me that making a different choice did not close the academic route I wanted.

11. How many postdocs?

There is no number of postdocs that is right for everybody. For some people one postdoc is enough before moving to a more permanent academic position, while for others a second or third postdoc opens up a new field, collaborator, country or research opportunity that they actively want.

Some researchers also simply like the postdoc life and choose to keep doing it for many years because they value the freedom, the large amount of research time, the relatively small amount of administration, and the possibility of moving between groups and countries. If that way of organising your career and life suits you, there is nothing inherently wrong with doing several postdocs.

For other people, temporary contracts and repeated moves become increasingly costly because they want to stay close to friends or family, build a community, buy a house, have more financial security, or simply stop organising life around the next two-year contract. What matters is whether the next postdoc fits what you actually want from both your work and your life.

If you are considering another one, I like the question:

What will this particular postdoc give me that I do not have now?

12. Start thinking about the next step earlier than feels necessary

Because postdoc contracts have fixed end dates, it is useful to find out well in advance how hiring works in the countries and fields you care about. European hiring is not synchronised, so vacancies can appear throughout the year. If you are aiming for an assistant-professor position in the Netherlands, I would follow AcademicTransfer and also look through older vacancies to get a realistic idea of what departments expect. Many older advertisements can still be read if the vacancy text was published directly on AcademicTransfer; if the listing only linked to a university website, that page has often disappeared after the deadline. These real job advertisements can tell you more about the portfolio you will need than generic career advice.

I would also not be afraid to apply for a position that comes along a little earlier than you had planned. Particularly in a small country such as the Netherlands, there is no guarantee that a similarly attractive vacancy will appear a year or two later, when your postdoc contract happens to be ending. If you find a position in a department and a place where you can really imagine building your career and your life, I would apply even if the timing is not quite ideal. Start dates are sometimes negotiable, and the amount of flexibility varies enormously: some departments have almost none, while others can accommodate a considerably later start. You will only find out by asking.

If you are considering industry, government, a research institute, healthcare, entrepreneurship or something else, the postdoc is also a good time to explore those options, talk to people who made those moves, and work out which parts of your research experience are useful there. Moving outside academia after a postdoc can be a very good next step, depending on what you want from your career.

Ten questions I would ask before accepting a postdoc

  1. What do I want to learn or build during this postdoc?

  2. Which research will actually be mine to lead?

  3. How much freedom will I have to start my own projects and collaborations?

  4. What can I continue working on after I leave?

  5. Who will I interact with besides the formal supervisor?

  6. How much teaching, supervision and service is expected?

  7. How long is the contract, and is the funding secure for that whole period?

  8. What research and travel budget is available?

  9. Where did previous postdocs go, and can I talk to one of them?

  10. What will I have learned or built by the end of this job?

If I had to condense this page

  • Know what you want the postdoc to give you, and use it to learn things that you could not already do at the end of the PhD.

  • Choose a mentor who can teach you something and who leaves enough room for your own questions and collaborations.

  • Try to leave the postdoc with research that is recognisably yours, while making good use of the unusually large amount of research time this stage can offer.

  • If you want a faculty career, use some of the time to build the teaching, supervision, grant-writing and service experience that will help with the next step, without letting those activities take over the postdoc.

  • Learn from the whole environment around you and build relationships that extend beyond one supervisor or one group.

  • Look seriously at fellowships and other ways of bringing your own funding when they fit what you want to do.

  • Take the life around the job seriously as well: moving for a postdoc affects your friends, family, hobbies, community and day-to-day life, and those things also influence how well you can work.

  • There is no fixed number of postdocs that makes sense for everybody; the useful question is what the next position will add to your work and your life.

  • If you are looking for a faculty position, study past vacancy advertisements and do not automatically pass up a good opportunity because it comes along earlier than expected.

Further reading and resources

  • Aaditya Ramdas, Why do a postdoc, and why do one at CMU?. A short and very personal case for using a postdoc as time for research, exploration and development. The second half is specifically about his own group, and some conclusions are explicitly his own view.

  • Philip E. Bourne and Iddo Friedberg, Ten Simple Rules for Selecting a Postdoctoral Position. Older, but still useful on choosing a mentor, learning something new and developing independence.

  • AcademicTransfer, postdoc career information. Dutch-oriented information on the postdoc stage and links to vacancies.

  • EURAXESS Jobs & Opportunities. Research jobs, funding opportunities and hosting offers across Europe and beyond.

  • NWO Rubicon. Check the current call for up-to-date eligibility and conditions.

  • Marie Skłodowska-Curie Actions Postdoctoral Fellowships. European postdoctoral fellowships centred on research, training and mobility.
